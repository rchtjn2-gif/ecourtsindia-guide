# Same name, wrong person: reading Indian court record checks

Searching Indian court records for a name is easy. Deciding which of the results actually belong to the person you are checking is the hard part. Court case records in India carry no Aadhaar or PAN field, and parties are recorded as typed by a court clerk. A careless check can attribute someone else's criminal case to a job candidate, tenant or prospective spouse.

## How big is the problem?

eCourtsIndia ran exact-phrase litigant searches for common two-word names across its index on 25 September 2026 and published the counts in its post on [namesakes in court record checks](https://blogs.ecourtsindia.com/2026/09/25/court-record-false-positive-same-name-india/). A few of the results:

| Name searched | Court records naming the litigant |
|---|---|
| Anil Kumar | 8,14,413 |
| Rajesh Kumar | 6,42,935 |
| Ramesh Kumar | 3,04,598 |
| Sunita Devi | 1,33,605 |
| Rahul Sharma | 31,276 |
| Ashok Patil | 17,786 (83% in Maharashtra) |
| Joseph Thomas | 3,432 (84% in Kerala) |

These are records, not people. One person with ten cases is ten records, and ten thousand different people called Anil Kumar are ten thousand people. A name search cannot tell the two apart.

## Spelling multiplies the problem

Transliteration means one person can appear under several spellings. The same post notes that Mohammad Salim, Mohd Salim, Md Salim and Mohammed Salim together return about 40,000 records, with each spelling clustering in different states. A check that searches only one spelling can miss real records; a check that merges spellings without more evidence creates false matches.

## Corroboration is not identity

Father's name, age and city help, but they are corroboration, not proof. eCourtsIndia's worked example describes two men whose names differ by one letter, who share a father's name and a birth year, but live in different cities. Each extra clue narrows the field; none of them alone settles it.

## How to read a check

A careful report separates results into levels:

- **Confirmed:** enough independent evidence that the record is the subject's. Only these should be treated as findings.
- **Probable / possible:** some matching details, not enough to attribute. Worth a closer look, not a conclusion.
- **Excluded:** shares the name but evidence shows it is someone else.

Two cautions from the same guidance:

1. For a person, **"clear" means nothing could be confirmed**, not that no case exists.
2. **Role matters.** A person who filed a case (petitioner or complainant) is a very different risk from a person a case was filed against.

## A practical checklist

- [ ] Search every likely spelling of the name.
- [ ] Narrow by state and district where the person has lived.
- [ ] Use father's name, age and address as corroboration together.
- [ ] Open the actual case record before drawing any conclusion.
- [ ] Note the person's role in each case.
- [ ] Record why each match was confirmed or excluded.

You can try a name, including your own, in [eCourtsIndia search](https://ecourtsindia.com/search); every result links back to the court record it comes from.
